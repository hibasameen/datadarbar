What is a mouza?

Every figure on the Rural Facilities layer counts mouzas. Almost nothing published about Pakistan uses this unit, so it is worth two minutes to know what is being counted — and what a share of mouzas can and cannot tell you.

A unit of land, not a settlement

A mouza is a revenue village: a bounded piece of land in the provincial land records, with a name, a fixed boundary and an area measured plot by plot. It is the smallest permanent unit of Pakistani land administration, and it long predates the state — most boundaries were drawn during colonial-era settlements and have barely moved since.

Because it is a unit of land, a mouza is not the same thing as a village. One mouza may contain several hamlets, a single large village, or nobody at all: 1,808 of them were recorded as unpopulated in 2020. Sizes vary enormously — a few hundred acres in canal-irrigated Punjab, hundreds of thousands in Balochistan.

Where it sits

The mouza is the bottom rung of the revenue hierarchy. Everything published on this site at tehsil level is, underneath, a bundle of these.

What the census does

The Mouza Census is run by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics with the provincial revenue departments, as a hundred-per-cent count rather than a sample. An enumerator goes to each mouza and records what is there: whether it has a girls' primary school, a basic health unit, a metalled street, mains electricity, a bazar, a police post. The 2020 round is the most recent; 2008 was the one before it.

It exists for two reasons. It supplies the sampling frame from which PBS draws rural household surveys, and it is the only source that describes rural facilities for the whole country at once — no survey, however large, visits every village.

How to read a percentage here

Places, not people

64.8% of mouzas have a girls' primary school. That is 30,554 mouzas out of 47,120 — not 64.8% of rural girls. A mouza of 12,000 people and a mouza of 300 each count exactly once. To say anything about people you would have to weight these by population, and the census does not carry any.

Presence, not access

The question is whether a facility sits inside the boundary. A school 500 metres over the line counts as absent; a school at the far end of a 40 km² mouza counts as present. Nothing here records distance, quality, staffing, or whether the building is in use.

Two kinds of share

Indicators ending in a plain percentage come from questions with mutually exclusive answers, so they sum to 100. Drinking water, health facility type, fuel and street surface are multiple response — one mouza can report three water sources — so those are divided by the reporting base and can legitimately exceed 100%.

Where it stops

The denominator is not published

PBS reports how many mouzas have a facility and how many do not, but never states how many were asked. Its own indicator blocks disagree: only 33 of the 544 enumerated tehsils give a single consistent base, and the blocks can differ by up to 181 mouzas. Every share on this site is therefore divided by the base implied by its own question, and where the blocks diverge by more than 5% the tehsil detail panel says so.

The rural edge is soft

The frame is rural by construction — a city is not a revenue village, so the built-up cores are simply absent, which is why 22 Karachi towns and cantonments and several city tehsils appear as no-data. But mouzas that urbanise stay in the frame: 4.9% are classified urban and a further 2.4% partly urban. Nationally that is small. In 49 tehsils it is above 20%, and in a handful — Model Town, Shalimar, Raiwind, Sukkur City — it is everything. PBS does not cross-tabulate the indicators by mouza status, so those cannot be separated out. The Settlement group in the indicator list shows each tehsil's exposure.

Three kinds of gap

Azad Jammu and Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan appear in the frame but were not enumerated in this round, so all 48 of their tehsils return nothing. Neither were Mand and Tump in Kech, or Kallag in Panjgur — three populated Makran sub-tehsils whose neighbours all report normally. And the census is a single snapshot: nothing here supports a claim about change over time.

Source: Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, Mouza Census 2020. The extraction, the tehsil crosswalk and a fuller account of the source's quirks are documented alongside the code.
